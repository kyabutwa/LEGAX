# LegaX — Privacy & Governance Architecture

## 22 — Privacy/Governance Architecture

**Canonical definition**

Privacy/Governance Architecture is the cross-cutting LegaX architecture that establishes how personal data, sensitive data, non-personal operational data, derived information, governance decisions, policies, responsibilities, rights, obligations, risks, exceptions, oversight, transparency, accountability, redress, and data-processing relationships are defined, controlled, evidenced, reviewed, and continuously improved across the LegaX ecosystem and its external processing environment.

It protects the agency, dignity, autonomy, confidentiality, legitimate interests, rights, expectations, and lawful participation of people while enabling communities, organizations, providers, workers, services, devices, systems, and intelligence to operate within explicit governance boundaries.

Privacy/Governance Architecture does **not** become a second Identity system, second Authorization system, second Administration system, second Security system, or parallel authority chain.

Privacy may constrain processing. Governance may establish policies, responsibilities, accountability, oversight and decision rights. Neither privacy nor governance may manufacture business authority that does not already exist through the canonical authority and authorization model.

**Core rule:**

**DATA/PROCESSING PURPOSE → GOVERNANCE BASIS → POLICY → CONTROL → AUTHORIZED PROCESSING → EVENT/EVIDENCE → REVIEW → ACCOUNTABILITY/REDRESS**

For consequential operations:

**IDENTITY → CONTEXT → GOVERNANCE CONDITIONS → AUTHORITY → AUTHORIZATION → ACTION → EVENT → EVIDENCE → PRIVACY/GOVERNANCE REVIEW**

## 1. Purpose

LegaX operates across identity, access, communities, organizations, providers, workers, services, buildings, units, facilities, devices, physical environments, payments, commerce, mobility, health, work and intelligence.

That means LegaX may encounter:

- identity and account information;
- authentication and credential information;
- participation and membership information;
- residential and location information;
- access and visitor information;
- biometric and other sensitive information;
- health-related information;
- financial and economic information;
- employment and professional information;

- operational and physical-world observations;
- provider and worker information;
- service usage and transaction information;
- event and evidence records;
- inferred, predicted, recommended or generated information;
- information received from external providers;
- information generated by LegaX itself.

Privacy/Governance Architecture ensures that the existence of this information does not imply unrestricted collection, use, sharing, inference, retention, surveillance, profiling, disclosure, or reuse.

The architecture must answer:

1. Why is this information processed?
2. What information is actually necessary?
3. Whose information is it?
4. Who is the data subject where applicable?
5. Who determines the purpose and means of processing?
6. Who processes it on whose behalf?
7. What legal, contractual, operational or other governance basis applies?
8. What notice and transparency are required?
9. What rights and choices apply?
10. What scope and context apply?
11. Who may access or use the information?
12. For what purpose may it be used?
13. May it be reused for another purpose?
14. Where may it be stored or transferred?
15. How long may it be retained?
16. What happens when the purpose ends?
17. What evidence demonstrates compliance?
18. What happens when governance conflicts with operational convenience?
19. How can a person challenge, correct, object to, restrict, delete or otherwise exercise applicable rights?
20. Who is accountable when something goes wrong?

## 2. Architectural distinction

Privacy/Governance Architecture combines two related but distinct concerns.

### Privacy

Privacy governs the legitimate, proportionate and accountable treatment of information relating to identifiable or identifiable persons and other information whose handling creates privacy, autonomy, dignity, safety or rights risks.

### Governance

Governance establishes the structures, policies, responsibilities, decision rights, oversight, accountability, review, exceptions, controls and remediation mechanisms through which LegaX determines how its architecture is operated responsibly.

Privacy therefore asks:

**“Should this information be collected, used, disclosed, retained, inferred or otherwise processed for this purpose and under these conditions?”**

Governance asks:

**“Who is responsible for determining, operating, reviewing, proving and correcting that processing?”**

Neither question replaces:

- Identity;
- Authentication;
- Account;
- Participation;
- Context;
- Role;
- Capability;
- Authority;
- Authorization;
- Access;
- Action;
- Event;
- Evidence;
- Security;
- Domain lifecycle.

## 3. Privacy is not secrecy

Privacy is broader than confidentiality.

Confidentiality protects information from unauthorized disclosure.

Privacy also concerns:

- purpose;
- necessity;
- proportionality;
- transparency;
- choice;
- autonomy;
- lawful processing;
- legitimate expectations;
- inference;
- profiling;
- aggregation;
- linkage;
- surveillance;
- retention;
- deletion;
- secondary use;
- sharing;
- transfer;
- redress;
- human rights;
- accountability.

Encrypted collection of unnecessary data is still unnecessary collection.

Authorized access to data does not make every subsequent use legitimate.

A valid business purpose does not automatically justify unlimited collection.

A technically possible inference is not automatically a permissible inference.

---

## 4. Governance is not administration

Phase 05 Administration remains the canonical system for managing authority structures, scopes, assignments, policies, delegation, configuration and operational governance.

Privacy/Governance Architecture does not replace it.

The distinction is:

**Governance → establishes/oversees rules, accountability, decision rights and stewardship.**

**Administration → manages governed authority structures and operational configuration.**

**Authorization → makes the runtime decision for a specific requested action.**

**Access → enforces an authorized interaction.**

A privacy policy cannot itself grant an administrator access to a database.

A governance board cannot cause an unauthorized action to become authorized merely by recording a resolution unless the governing authority and authorization model validly establish that authority.

## 5. Canonical privacy/governance chain

The canonical operating model is:

**PURPOSE → DATA/PROCESSING INVENTORY → GOVERNANCE BASIS → RISK ASSESSMENT → POLICY → CONTROL → AUTHORIZED PROCESSING → EVENT → EVIDENCE → REVIEW → REMEDIATION**

For a new processing activity:

**PROPOSE → IDENTIFY DATA → IDENTIFY PURPOSE → IDENTIFY ACTORS → DETERMINE ROLES → DETERMINE BASIS → ASSESS NECESSITY/PROPORTIONALITY → ASSESS RISK → DESIGN CONTROLS → REVIEW/APPROVE → IMPLEMENT → MONITOR → REVIEW → RETIRE**

For a rights request:

**REQUEST → AUTHENTICATE/VERIFY REQUESTER AS APPROPRIATE → LOCATE DATA → DETERMINE SCOPE/EXCEPTIONS → AUTHORIZE DISCLOSURE OR CHANGE → EXECUTE → RECORD → NOTIFY → CLOSE/ESCALATE**

Authentication of a rights requester is not authorization for unrelated account activity.

## 7. Controller and processor separation

Where applicable law distinguishes controllers and processors, LegaX must represent that distinction explicitly.

A controller determines purposes and means of processing within the applicable legal framework.

A processor processes personal data on behalf of a controller under the applicable contract and instructions.

A processor must not silently become a controller merely because it technically possesses the data.

A provider's possession of LegaX data does not itself establish ownership, authority, or unrestricted reuse rights.

Processor contracts should address:

- purpose;
- scope;
- documented instructions;
- confidentiality;
- security;
- subprocessors;
- assistance with rights;
- incident/breach support;
- audits/assurance;
- deletion or return;
- retention;
- transfers;
- jurisdiction;
- termination;
- evidence;
- permitted secondary processing.

The architecture must support cases where LegaX is:

- controller;
- processor;
- joint controller where legitimately established;
- recipient;
- integration participant;
- or merely a conduit that does not determine the relevant purpose.

The role must be determined per processing activity, not globally by brand.

## 10. Personal data boundary

Personal data should be interpreted according to applicable law and context.

LegaX must assume that data can become personal through:

- direct identification;
- indirect identification;
- linkage;
- pseudonymous identifiers;
- device identifiers;
- location;
- behavioral information;
- transaction history;
- household/community relationships;
- biometric information;
- derived profiles;
- combinations of otherwise non-identifying fields.

De-identification and pseudonymization reduce risk but must not be treated as magical deletion of privacy obligations.

Re-identification risk must be considered where aggregation, linkage or auxiliary information could identify a person.

---

## 11. Sensitive data

Sensitive information requires stronger governance where applicable.

Examples may include:

- biometric information;
- health information;
- genetic information;
- precise location;
- financial information;
- authentication secrets;
- children's information;
- information revealing highly private relationships or characteristics;
- information whose misuse could create material safety, discrimination, dignity or rights harm.

Sensitive processing should require stronger justification, minimization, access restrictions, security, retention controls, monitoring and review where appropriate.

Sensitive classification does not itself prohibit processing.

Conversely, absence of a sensitive label does not make risky processing automatically acceptable.

---

## 12. Purpose limitation

Every material processing activity must have an explicit purpose.

A purpose should be:

- specific enough to govern use;
- legitimate;
- documented;
- understandable;
- linked to the service or operation;
- bounded by scope and time;
- reviewed when materially changed.

Data collected for one purpose must not silently become a universal dataset for unrelated purposes.

Secondary use requires its own governance analysis.

Purpose expansion must trigger:

**NEW PURPOSE → COMPATIBILITY/LEGAL BASIS REVIEW → RISK REVIEW → NOTICE/CHOICE REVIEW → CONTROL UPDATE → APPROVAL → EFFECTIVE DATE**

Purpose change is not merely a product configuration change.

---

## 13. Data minimization

LegaX must process only data reasonably necessary for the defined purpose.

Minimization applies to:

- collection;
- fields;
- precision;
- frequency;
- retention;
- access;
- copies;
- exports;
- model inputs;
- logs;
- analytics;
- backups;
- test environments;
- derived data;
- provider sharing.

The question is not:

**“Can LegaX collect this?”**

The question is:

**“What is the minimum information and precision reasonably necessary to achieve the legitimate purpose?”**

For location, for example, a coarse area may be sufficient where precise coordinates are unnecessary.

For access, a decision result may be sufficient where the operator does not need the underlying identity evidence.

For analytics, aggregated or pseudonymized data may be sufficient where individual-level data is unnecessary.

Derived information includes:

- classifications;
- risk scores;
- predictions;
- recommendations;
- inferred relationships;
- inferred location patterns;
- inferred preferences;
- fraud indicators;
- behavioral profiles;
- eligibility suggestions;
- model-generated summaries;

Inference cannot silently become a canonical identity attribute, authority, relationship, eligibility fact or adverse finding.

Potential triggers include:

## 19. Consent

Consent is one possible governance basis where applicable; it is not a universal basis for every processing operation.

Where consent is relied upon, LegaX should represent:

- who consented;
- what was presented;
- purpose;
- data categories;
- recipients where relevant;
- time;
- version;
- method;
- scope;
- withdrawal;
- status;
- evidence;
- consequences of withdrawal.

Consent must not be fabricated from account creation, silence, technical access, or unrelated acceptance.

Withdrawal must be represented as a governed event and must trigger the processing consequences defined by the applicable purpose and law.

Withdrawal does not necessarily erase historical processing that was lawful at the time, and it does not necessarily override independent legal obligations.

## 20. Legal/governance basis

LegaX must represent the applicable basis for processing rather than reducing every activity to “consent.”

Depending on jurisdiction and processing context, a basis may include:

- consent;
- contract;
- legal obligation;
- legitimate interests;
- vital interests;
- public interest;
- research/statistical grounds;
- other legally recognized bases.

The exact available bases and requirements are jurisdiction-specific.

A product feature cannot invent a legal basis.

A governance record cannot convert unlawful processing into lawful processing.

## 21. Data-subject rights

Where applicable, LegaX must support rights-management workflows, including relevant rights such as:

- information/transparency;
- access;
- correction/rectification;
- objection;
- deletion/erasure where applicable;
- restriction where applicable;
- portability where applicable;
- withdrawal of consent where applicable;
- review or safeguards concerning automated decision-making where applicable;
- complaint and redress.

Rights are not necessarily absolute and may have lawful exceptions.

A rights request must therefore follow a governed process:

**REQUEST → REQUESTER VERIFICATION → SCOPE IDENTIFICATION → LEGAL/PROCESSING ANALYSIS → DECISION → EXECUTION → EVIDENCE → RESPONSE → ESCALATION/REDRESS**

Kenya's ODPC identifies rights including information, access, objection, correction and deletion of false or misleading data, while the applicable law and regulations define further procedures and exceptions. citeturn1search0turn1search28

A rights request must not expose unrelated people's information.

## 23. Automated decision-making

LegaX must distinguish:

**automation that assists a decision**

from

**automation that makes or materially determines a decision.**

High-impact automated decisions require stronger governance where applicable, including:

- purpose;
- data quality;
- model/process provenance;
- validation;
- fairness/impact assessment;
- explainability appropriate to context;
- human oversight where required;
- contestability;
- appeal/redress;
- monitoring;
- drift management;
- rollback;
- auditability.

AI recommendation ≠ decision.

Automated decision ≠ authorization automatically.

A model cannot create authority merely because its output is confident.

## 25. Biometric privacy

Biometric processing is a high-risk capability in LegaX and must be explicitly governed.

Biometric sequence:

**CAPTURE → QUALITY/ANTI-SPOOFING → PROCESSING → MATCH/ASSERTION → AUTHENTICATION RESULT → AUTHORIZATION → ACCESS/ACTION**

A biometric result is not authority.

Biometric processing must identify:

- purpose;
- modality;
- processor;
- storage architecture;
- template/sample handling;
- retention;
- matching location;
- liveness/anti-spoofing;
- alternatives;
- user communication;
- legal basis;
- access;
- deletion;
- provider involvement;
- incident handling.

Where device-local biometrics are used, the LegaX architecture should prefer receiving an appropriate authentication result rather than unnecessarily receiving raw biometric material.

Palm/hand recognition requires suitable hardware and governed provider capability; a generic device capability must never be assumed.

## 32. Retention, deletion and archival

Every material processing activity should have a retention rule.

Retention should consider:

- purpose;
- legal obligation;
- contractual requirement;
- dispute;
- security investigation;
- evidence preservation;
- regulatory requirement;
- operational need;
- backup lifecycle;
- archival;
- deletion feasibility.

Canonical lifecycle:

**ACTIVE → RETENTION-ELIGIBLE → ARCHIVE/RESTRICT → DELETION-ELIGIBLE → DELETE/ANONYMIZE → VERIFY → EVIDENCE**

Deletion must be technically meaningful where required, including consideration of replicas, caches, derived datasets and downstream processors where applicable.

Legal hold or legitimate preservation may suspend deletion for defined scope and duration.

A legal hold must not become permanent hidden retention.

## 33. Data correction and quality

Privacy depends on data quality.

Material personal data should preserve:

- source;
- collection time;
- update time;
- verification status;
- confidence where appropriate;
- correction history;
- supersession;
- dispute status.

A correction must not silently rewrite historical evidence that is legitimately required for accountability.

Instead:

**CLAIM → CORRECTION REQUEST → VALIDATION → NEW VALUE → SUPERSESSION/REVISION → EVENT → EVIDENCE**

Accuracy does not mean rewriting history to make history look accurate.

## 34. Data provenance

Every material data element or dataset should be traceable enough to answer:

- where did it originate?
- who supplied it?
- when?
- by which process?
- under which purpose?
- under which provider?
- through which adapter?
- was it declared, observed, verified, inferred or proposed?
- was it transformed?
- was it merged?
- who changed it?
- what policy governed the transformation?

Provenance connects Privacy/Governance Architecture to Phase 11, 17 and 18.

**PROVENANCE ≠ TRUTH**

Provenance explains origin and transformation; it does not automatically establish correctness.

## 35. Data lineage

LegaX should support lineage:

**SOURCE → COLLECTION → TRANSFORMATION → STORAGE → PROCESSING → DERIVATION → SHARING → OUTPUT → RETENTION/DELETION**

Lineage is especially important for:

- sensitive information;
- identity;
- financial data;
- health data;
- biometric data;
- AI inputs/outputs;
- analytics;
- regulatory reporting;
- evidence.

A derived dataset should not become impossible to trace back to its governance conditions.

## 36. Access to data

Data access follows the canonical authorization model.

The data subject relationship, role, capability, authority, purpose, context, policy, security, lifecycle and target must be evaluated.

Privacy filtering may further reduce the data returned.

Therefore:

**AUTHORIZATION → DATA ACCESS DECISION → MINIMIZATION/FILTERING → ACCESS/RETRIEVAL → EVENT/EVIDENCE**

A valid authorization does not necessarily mean the caller receives every field.

The minimum necessary representation should be preferred.

## 37. Purpose-bound data access

Where possible, data access should carry purpose metadata.

Example:

**Actor → Purpose → Dataset → Fields → Operation → Scope → Time → Policy → Authorization**

The purpose should be machine-readable where feasible.

A purpose label is not itself authorization.

A request claiming “security” or “analytics” must still satisfy the applicable authority and governance rules.

**SECURITY ≠ PRIVACY**

A system can be secure and still process too much personal data.

A system can have strong encryption and still use data for an impermissible purpose.

A privacy requirement can require less collection even when additional collection would be technically secure.

## 40. Governance policy lifecycle

Privacy and governance policies follow Phase 10 Lifecycle & Policy.

Canonical lifecycle:

**DRAFT → REVIEW → IMPACT ASSESS → APPROVAL → EFFECTIVE → MONITORED → AMENDED/SUSPENDED → RETIRED**

- policy ID;
- version;
- owner;
- scope;
- jurisdiction;
- purpose;
- effective time;
- expiry/review time;
- dependencies;
- exceptions;
- enforcement mapping;
- evidence requirements.

## 41. Governance decision rights

- required approvals;
- conflicts;
- effective time;
- expiry/review;
- evidence;
- appeal/escalation.

Governance decision ≠ runtime authorization.

A governance body can establish a policy, but a runtime request must still be evaluated against the current applicable policy, authority and context.

## 42. Separation of duties

Privacy governance should prevent one actor from unilaterally:

- defining a high-risk processing purpose;
- approving it;
- implementing it;
- reviewing its compliance;
- deleting evidence of noncompliance;
- adjudicating their own exception;
- approving their own high-impact data export.

Where risk warrants:

**PROPOSE → REVIEW → APPROVE → IMPLEMENT → MONITOR → ASSESS**

The exact separation depends on scope and risk.

SoD is a governance control, not a universal prohibition on one person performing multiple tasks.

## 86. Canonical final rule

**Privacy/Governance Architecture ensures that LegaX processes information and operates governance structures only for defined, justified, proportionate, accountable and appropriately authorized purposes, while preserving data-subject rights, privacy, transparency, security, evidence, oversight, redress, jurisdictional compliance and human agency throughout the information lifecycle.**

It does not become a second IAM, second Authorization system, second Security system, or parallel authority chain.

**Privacy may constrain processing. Governance may establish responsibility, policy, oversight and accountability. Neither may manufacture authority.**

**NO GOVERNED PURPOSE/BASIS → NO UNCONTROLLED PROCESSING.**

**NO AUTHORIZATION → NO CONSEQUENTIAL LEGAX ACTION.**

**PRIVACY/GOVERNANCE PROTECTS PEOPLE AND THE LEGITIMATE USE OF DATA WITHOUT BECOMING A PARALLEL AUTHORITY SYSTEM.**

**Status:** Architectural contract — implementation follows only after this gate and its dependent contracts are satisfied.
